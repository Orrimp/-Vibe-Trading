### [1] Time Series Momentum
- **Authors / Venue:** Tobias J. Moskowitz, Yao Hua Ooi, Lasse Heje Pedersen / Journal of Financial Economics 104(2)
- **Year:** 2012
- **Source:** SSRN:2089463 ; PDF https://w4.stern.nyu.edu/facdir/lpederse/papers/TimeSeriesMomentum.pdf
- **% read:** 50% (abstract + method + headline results; primary PDF was binary-encoded, method/results cross-read via Quantpedia + AQR summaries)
- **Summary:** Documents "time series momentum" (a.k.a. absolute/trend momentum): a security's own past 12-month excess return positively predicts its next-month return across 58 diverse futures/forwards (commodities, currencies, equity indices, bonds), 1965–2009. Signal = sign of trailing 12m excess return; positions sized **inversely to ex-ante volatility** (GARCH in the paper; simple realized-vol works too); monthly rebalance. A diversified, vol-scaled TSM portfolio earns ~1.31 Sharpe with large alpha and little factor exposure, and performs **best in extreme up/down markets** (a partial crisis hedge). The trend persists ~12 months then partially reverses over longer horizons; speculators profit at hedgers' expense.
- **Relevance to our system:** Core, directly testable. Our advisor is single-coin, so this is the *time-series* (own-history) flavour, not cross-sectional — exactly the regime a single asset lives in. Two transferable design rules: (1) the signal is just the sign of a trailing return over a lookback; (2) **volatility scaling of position size is the load-bearing ingredient** of the reported Sharpe, more than the entry rule. BUT: results are pre-cost, futures-based, and diversified across 58 instruments — on ONE crypto coin with real fees/slippage and no diversification, expect far less. Good baseline to bake off against buy-and-hold; watch for the well-documented post-2009 decay.

### [2] Returns to Buying Winners and Selling Losers: Implications for Stock Market Efficiency
- **Authors / Venue:** Narasimhan Jegadeesh, Sheridan Titman / Journal of Finance 48(1)
- **Year:** 1993
- **Source:** DOI:10.1111/j.1540-6261.1993.tb04702.x (primary paywalled; method/results cross-read via Foxholm research summary)
- **% read:** 50% (abstract + full method + headline results via authoritative secondary; primary behind JSTOR/Wiley paywall)
- **Summary:** The seminal *cross-sectional* momentum paper. Rank all NYSE/AMEX stocks into deciles by trailing return over a formation period J ∈ {3,6,9,12}m, hold K ∈ {3,6,9,12}m; buy top-decile winners, short bottom-decile losers, equal-weighted (a 4×4 grid). All 16 J/K combos earn significant positive returns ~0.9–1.3%/month; best is J=12,K=3 at ~1.31%/month. Profits dissipate after ~12 months and **partially reverse beyond ~2 years** (long-horizon mean reversion). Directly contradicts the efficient-market hypothesis. Caveats the authors/literature flag: costs excluded, short-sale frictions ignored, survivorship bias, and later-documented "momentum crashes" in market rebounds.
- **Relevance to our system:** Background-leaning for us — cross-sectional momentum needs a *universe* to rank (long winners / short losers), which a single-coin advisor does not have. But two ideas transfer: (1) the formation-vs-holding (J/K) parameter grid is exactly the kind of thing our bake-off sweeps, and the lesson is that the *effect* is robust to the exact J/K (don't overfit the window); (2) the long-horizon reversal warns that a momentum signal tuned on a trending window will mean-revert — our robustness gate (bootstrap + buy-hold benchmark) is the right defence. Net: informs *why* momentum exists, less *how* to deploy it on one coin.

### [3] Optimal Execution of Portfolio Transactions (Almgren–Chriss model)
- **Authors / Venue:** Robert Almgren, Neil Chriss / Journal of Risk 3(2) (2000/2001); read via the RL-extension paper by Hendricks & Wilcox that restates the original model
- **Year:** 2000 (read via arXiv:1403.2229, 2014)
- **Source:** arXiv:1403.2229 (RL extension restating the AC framework); original DOI 10.21314/JOR.2001.041
- **% read:** 50% (full method/intuition of the AC model via the restating paper + its RL extension and result)
- **Summary:** The AC model frames liquidating a fixed position over a fixed horizon as minimizing **E[cost] + λ·Var[cost]**, where λ is risk aversion. Price impact is split into **permanent** (lasting, linear in cumulative trade) and **temporary** (transient, linear in trade *rate*) components. Solving yields an **efficient frontier** trading execution speed vs cost-variance: a risk-neutral trader (λ→0) spreads trades evenly (→ TWAP-like), while a risk-averse trader **front-loads** to cut exposure to volatility, producing an exponentially decaying trade schedule. The RL extension (Hendricks & Wilcox 2014) learns a data-driven policy on top of the AC trajectory to adapt to non-linear/stochastic impact, reporting improvements over the static schedule.
- **Relevance to our system:** Background/medium. We don't liquidate large blocks, but the AC decomposition is the right *mental model for slippage* in our cost engine: a per-trade temporary-impact term (proportional to size/ADV) plus a tiny permanent term. For an honest single-coin sim with €200, impact is negligible, so the practical takeaway is the reverse: our cost realism should center on **fees + bid-ask spread**, not impact — AC tells us when impact matters (large size vs liquidity), and €200 is firmly in the "doesn't" regime. The risk-vs-cost frontier idea also justifies *not* trading too fast/often: every rebalance pays spread.

### [4] Pairs Trading: Performance of a Relative-Value Arbitrage Rule
- **Authors / Venue:** Evan Gatev, William N. Goetzmann, K. Geert Rouwenhorst / Review of Financial Studies 19(3)
- **Year:** 2006 (orig. working paper 1998)
- **Source:** SSRN:141615 ; method/results cross-read via Quantpedia
- **% read:** 50% (abstract + full method + key results & decay/cost findings via secondary; primary PDF host refused connection)
- **Summary:** The canonical *distance-method* pairs-trading study. Formation: over 12 months, normalize each stock's price to a cumulative-return index and pick the 20 pairs with the smallest sum of squared deviations between the two normalized series. Trade over the next 6 months: open a long-short (long the laggard, short the leader) when the spread diverges by **2 standard deviations** of its formation-period spread, close on convergence. On NYSE/AMEX/NASDAQ 1962–2002 this earned ~11% annualized excess return / ~0.81%/month, Sharpe ≈ 1.22, **after estimated transaction costs**, with low market beta (market-neutral). Crucially, follow-up work (Do & Faff; Chen et al.) shows the effect has **steadily decayed** as more capital chased it — consistent with adaptive-market efficiency.
- **Relevance to our system:** Background for us — pairs trading is inherently *two-asset* and market-neutral; a single-coin advisor cannot run it as-is. It earns a place because (1) it is the textbook example of a **mean-reversion** strategy with an explicit, testable z-score entry/exit (±2σ on a spread) — the same band logic applies to mean-reversion on ONE coin's own deviation from a moving average (Bollinger-style), which IS in our strategy set; and (2) it is a clean cautionary tale that even a Sharpe-1.2, cost-survived, peer-reviewed edge **decays toward zero** once crowded — reinforcing our validated thesis and the need for the robustness gate. A future multi-coin extension could revisit true pairs/cointegration.

### [5] High-Frequency Trading in a Limit Order Book (Avellaneda–Stoikov)
- **Authors / Venue:** Marco Avellaneda, Sasha Stoikov / Quantitative Finance 8(3)
- **Year:** 2008
- **Source:** DOI:10.1080/14697680701381228 ; PDF https://people.orie.cornell.edu/sfs33/LimitOrderBook.pdf
- **% read:** 75% (full method + both key results + inventory intuition from the primary PDF; skipped some asymptotic-derivation algebra)
- **Summary:** The foundational *market-making* model. Mid-price is a Brownian motion; market buy/sell orders arrive as Poisson processes whose intensity decays exponentially the further the quote sits from mid (param k). The maker maximizes exponential (CARA, risk-aversion γ) utility of terminal cash+inventory over horizon T. Two closed-form results: (1) a **reservation/indifference price** = mid minus an inventory- and risk-dependent skew (≈ q·γ·σ²·(T−t)), so quotes shift *against* the current inventory to mean-revert holdings toward zero; (2) an **optimal total spread** ≈ γσ²(T−t) + (2/γ)·ln(1+γ/k) — wider with higher risk aversion, volatility, and time remaining; tighter with higher order-arrival intensity. Net effect: symmetric spread around a *skewed* reservation price actively controls inventory risk vs spread capture.
- **Relevance to our system:** Background only for our current scope. Market making requires posting two-sided limit quotes and capturing spread tick-by-tick — our advisor is a *taker* paper-sim that decides long/flat on one coin, not a quoting engine, and we have no live order book. The transferable nugget is conceptual: the **inventory-skew idea** (push your target exposure back toward neutral as a function of vol and risk aversion) is a principled alternative to fixed position sizing, and the spread formula is a reminder that **volatility directly widens the cost of being in the market** — supporting a vol-aware sizing/throttle rule. Not a strategy to bake off now; relevant if we ever add a maker/limit-order execution mode.

### [6] On the Properties of Equally-Weighted Risk Contribution Portfolios (Risk Parity)
- **Authors / Venue:** Sébastien Maillard, Thierry Roncalli, Jérôme Teiletche / Journal of Portfolio Management 36(4)
- **Year:** 2010
- **Source:** SSRN:1271972 ; PDF https://fileserver.carloalberto.org/cerp/WP_142.pdf
- **% read:** 50% (abstract + definitions + key theoretical properties + empirical takeaways via primary PDF extract)
- **Summary:** Formalizes the **Equal Risk Contribution (ERC)** / risk-parity portfolio: choose weights so each asset's *risk contribution* (weight × marginal contribution to portfolio volatility) is equal. Proven properties: ERC always exists, is unique, and sits **between the minimum-variance and the equal-weight portfolio** in concentration. Key closed form: when all pairwise correlations are equal, **ERC weights are inversely proportional to each asset's volatility** (1/σ weighting). Empirically ERC delivers better risk-adjusted returns (higher Sharpe) and smaller drawdowns than equal-weight, and is more robust/less estimation-sensitive than full mean-variance optimization (which needs fragile expected-return and full-covariance inputs).
- **Relevance to our system:** Background for the single-coin case (no cross-asset weights to balance), but **high relevance if/when we go multi-coin**, and one idea ports today. The 1/σ result is the multi-asset cousin of the **inverse-vol position sizing** that recurs in TSM [1] and AS [5]: scale exposure down when the asset is more volatile. For one coin that becomes a **vol-target throttle** on the single position (already part of our v3 vol-overlay work). The deeper lesson is methodological: ERC is popular precisely because it *avoids estimating expected returns* — echoing our thesis that return forecasts are the fragile part. Bake-off design takeaway: prefer sizing rules driven by (estimable) volatility over (unestimable) return forecasts.

### [7] Value and Momentum Everywhere
- **Authors / Venue:** Clifford S. Asness, Tobias J. Moskowitz, Lasse Heje Pedersen / Journal of Finance 68(3)
- **Year:** 2013
- **Source:** SSRN:2174501 ; DOI:10.1111/jofi.12021
- **% read:** 50% (abstract + full method + key correlation/factor findings via authoritative secondaries; primary paywalled, no Sharpe magnitudes recovered)
- **Summary:** Shows that **value** and **momentum** premia exist consistently across 8 markets/asset classes — individual stocks in US/UK/Europe/Japan plus country index, bond, currency, and commodity futures — with consistent signal definitions (value: e.g. book/price, real rates, futures-curve slope; momentum: trailing ~12m return). The headline result: value and momentum are **negatively correlated** (both within and across asset classes), so a 50/50 combination has a **markedly higher Sharpe** than either alone (true diversification, not just averaging). Returns load on common *global* value and momentum factors, and a global three-factor model with **funding-liquidity risk** partially explains them — momentum crashes cluster in funding-stress periods exactly when value recovers.
- **Relevance to our system:** Medium-conceptual; not directly deployable on one coin (value needs a fundamental anchor like book value or carry, which a single crypto lacks a clean analogue for). The transferable insight is the most important in the paper: **combine negatively-correlated signals.** For us that means a momentum/trend rule and a mean-reversion rule are natural opposites — trend wins in trends, mean-reversion wins in ranges — so a regime-blend or ensemble could be more robust than either standalone, which is a concrete bake-off experiment. Also reinforces [2]/[4]: momentum "crashes" in stress, so any momentum candidate must be stress-tested (our bootstrap gate covers this).

### [8] The Kelly Criterion (Understanding the Kelly Criterion / Kelly 1956)
- **Authors / Venue:** J. L. Kelly Jr. (1956, Bell System Technical Journal); exposition by Edward O. Thorp (2008) and standard references
- **Year:** 1956 (Thorp exposition 2008)
- **Source:** Kelly DOI:10.1002/j.1538-7305.1956.tb03809.x ; Thorp PDF https://rybn.org/halloffame/PDFS/2008_Understanding_Kelly_New.pdf (binary); formula/caveats cross-read via en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kelly_criterion
- **% read:** 50% (formulas + practical caveats via Wikipedia + WebSearch of Thorp; primary Bell paper and Thorp PDF not read as text)
- **Summary:** Kelly sizing chooses the bet/position fraction that **maximizes expected log wealth**, equivalently the long-run **geometric growth rate**. Discrete form: f* = (bp − q)/b = p − q/b (p win prob, q=1−p, b payoff odds). Continuous/investing form: **f* = (μ − r)/σ²** — expected excess return over variance. Properties: it provably maximizes long-run growth and (asymptotically) minimizes time to a wealth target, **but** full Kelly is extremely volatile with deep interim drawdowns. Practitioners use **fractional Kelly** (commonly half-Kelly): a half-Kelly bet keeps ~3/4 of the growth rate for ~1/2 the variance, a strongly favorable trade. **Overbetting beyond full Kelly lowers growth AND raises ruin risk**, and Kelly is acutely sensitive to estimation error in p/μ — overestimating edge pushes you past optimal.
- **Relevance to our system:** **High — directly actionable as a position-sizing primitive.** The continuous form f* = μ/σ² is the rigorous justification for **vol-scaled sizing** (the recurring theme in [1][6]) and connects sizing to an explicit (if fragile) edge estimate. Practical guidance for our advisor: (1) if we ever size by an estimated edge, use **fractional (½ or ¼) Kelly**, never full — full Kelly's drawdowns are unacceptable for a retail €200 advisor and estimation error makes full Kelly dangerous; (2) the μ/σ² form makes explicit that **the σ² (vol) input is estimable but the μ (return) input is not** — so a vol-target (fix σ², drop the μ forecast) is the robust degenerate case, matching our thesis. Strong candidate to encode as the sizing math behind a vol-target overlay, with an e2e divergence test per our non-negotiable.

### [9] Fundamentals of Perpetual Futures
- **Authors / Venue:** Songrun He, Asaf Manela, Omri Ross, Victor von Wachter / arXiv working paper (later QJE-track)
- **Year:** 2022
- **Source:** arXiv:2212.06888
- **% read:** 50% (abstract + mechanism + no-arbitrage relationships + empirical summary via the arXiv abstract page)
- **Summary:** Defines the **perpetual future** (a non-expiring leveraged derivative) and the **funding-rate** mechanism that keeps the perp pegged to spot: longs periodically pay shorts (or vice versa) an amount proportional to the perp-minus-spot gap, so the funding sign tracks whether the perp trades rich or cheap. Derives **no-arbitrage perp prices** in frictionless markets and price *bounds* under trading costs; the implied trade is **cash-and-carry**: long spot + short perp captures the funding stream when the perp is rich. Empirically, crypto perps show **larger no-arbitrage deviations than traditional FX**, those deviations co-move across coins and shrink over time, and the implied arbitrage earns **high Sharpe ratios** — but persistent deviations imply real frictions (liquidation, funding-sign flips) cap exploitation.
- **Relevance to our system:** **Potentially the single most relevant *crypto-native* edge** — and notable because it is NOT a price-prediction strategy. Funding-rate / basis arbitrage (long spot + short perp) earns a **near-market-neutral carry** independent of coin direction, with reported Sharpes far above any price-momentum rule (cross-confirmed by the carry-trade search: ~6.45 Sharpe 2020–2025, ~8% mean funding return at ~0.8% vol). For our advisor this is a genuinely different lever from the trend/mean-reversion set, and it directly fits the "no active *directional* strategy beats holding" thesis — the carry edge is *structural*, not predictive. CAVEATS before we trust it: (1) it requires **shorting a perp** (margin, liquidation risk), which our current long/flat spot-only paper-sim does not model; (2) funding flips sign in bear/neutral regimes, so the carry is not free; (3) realistic perp fees + funding-payment frequency + liquidation buffers must be simulated. Flagged as a high-value future feature: a *carry/basis* mode would need a perp+margin model added to the engine.

### [10] A Trend Factor for the Cross-Section of Cryptocurrency Returns (CTREND)
- **Authors / Venue:** Christian Fieberg, Gerrit Liedtke, Thorsten Poddig, Thomas Walker, Adam Zaremba / Journal of Financial and Quantitative Analysis
- **Year:** 2025 (SSRN 2023)
- **Source:** SSRN:4601972 ; DOI:10.1017/S0022109024000747
- **% read:** 50% (abstract + method + headline results & robustness via authoritative search summary; primary PDF binary)
- **Summary:** Builds **CTREND**, a trend factor for the crypto cross-section that aggregates **28 technical signals** (moving averages over many lookbacks, momentum oscillators, volume- and volatility-based indicators) into one signal via machine learning, on >3,000 coins. CTREND **reliably predicts the cross-section of returns**, cannot be subsumed by the known crypto market/size/momentum factors, and — the key claim — **outperforms the standard momentum factor by >9× in Sharpe with less downside risk**. The authors stress it is **robust across sub-periods, market conditions, and alternative designs, and survives transaction costs**.
- **Relevance to our system:** Medium — strongest as a *signal-design* lesson, weakest as a deployable component (it's cross-sectional over 3,000 coins; we have one). Two takeaways: (1) **combining many lookbacks/indicators into one aggregate trend signal** beats any single moving-average rule and is more robust — for our single coin this argues for an *ensemble/composed* trend signal (multiple MAs blended) rather than one tuned SMA, which fits our existing composed-family sweep engine. (2) It is one of the few studies claiming **post-cost, cross-sub-period robustness**, so it's a useful counterweight to the "nothing survives" thesis — BUT the survival is *cross-sectional and ML-aggregated*; whether a single-coin time-series analogue survives is exactly the open question our bake-off should test. Caution: ML aggregation of 28 signals on a wide panel is a known overfitting hazard; the cross-sectional breadth (3,000 coins) is doing a lot of the work that a single coin can't replicate.

### [11] Using Algorithmic Trading to Analyze Short-Term Profitability of Bitcoin
- **Authors / Venue:** Israa Ali et al. / PeerJ Computer Science (open access, PMC7959592)
- **Year:** 2021
- **Source:** PMC:PMC7959592 ; DOI:10.7717/peerj-cs.337
- **% read:** 75% (full method + results table + cost analysis from the open-access HTML)
- **Summary:** Benchmarks **16 algorithmic strategies** — reservation-price (online-algorithm) rules and variable/fixed-length **moving-average** rules — against buy-and-hold on Bitcoin over 15–30 day windows. **Gross of fees, all 16 beat buy-and-hold** (e.g. best RP* earns ~10.1% over 30 days vs B&H −9.1%). The decisive result is the **cost sensitivity**: at a 4% round-trip fee, **no strategy is profitable over 15 days** and only 2 of 16 survive over 30 days; at 2% fees, profitable strategies drop from 11 to 5. Moving-average rules beat B&H but not each other reliably.
- **Relevance to our system:** **Direct, high-value confirmation of our core thesis** on the *exact* asset class. This is a single-coin (BTC) study showing the precise failure mode our advisor must guard against: technical rules look great **gross** and **collapse net of realistic costs**. Concrete design imperatives: (1) our cost model's fee/spread assumption is *the* decision variable — a strategy that wins at 0.1% fees can flip to a loss at 1% all-in cost, so the bake-off must report **post-cost** numbers and run a fee-sensitivity sweep; (2) frequent-rebalancing strategies (short-window MA crossovers) are most exposed — penalize turnover; (3) the "beats B&H gross" result is nearly worthless as a ranking criterion — only post-cost vs buy-and-hold matters, which our frozen gate already enforces. Excellent citation for the presenter deck when explaining *why* the advisor recommends holding.

### [12] Risks and Returns of Cryptocurrency
- **Authors / Venue:** Yukun Liu, Aleh Tsyvinski / Review of Financial Studies 34(6); NBER WP 24877
- **Year:** 2018 (RFS 2021)
- **Source:** NBER WP 24877 (PDF https://www.nber.org/system/files/working_papers/w24877/w24877.pdf) ; SSRN:3226952
- **% read:** 50% (abstract + headline momentum/quintile results & predictor list via NBER abstract + WebSearch of the WP; PDF not read as text)
- **Summary:** Studies BTC/ETH/XRP and the crypto market return. Two main findings: (1) crypto returns are **not well explained by traditional equity/macro/currency risk factors** — they are a distinct asset class; (2) there is a **strong time-series momentum effect** plus **investor-attention proxies** (Google search, social-media post counts, and negative attention like "Bitcoin hack") that predict future returns. In weekly quintile sorts, the top-momentum quintile earns ~**11.22%/week** (Sharpe 0.45) vs the bottom quintile ~2.60%/week (Sharpe 0.19) over 1–4 week horizons. Momentum and attention are the robust predictors; standard factors are not.
- **Relevance to our system:** Medium-high and nuanced. It is academic support that **crypto time-series momentum is real** (supports baking off a TSM rule [1]) and that **investor-attention/sentiment** is a genuine crypto-specific predictor — a candidate *feature* if we ever add an exogenous data source (search trends, social volume) beyond price. BUT three cautions for our advisor: (1) these are **gross, weekly, cross-sectional quintile** spreads over many coins — the huge 11%/week is a long-short top-minus-bottom number across a universe, NOT what a single coin's own momentum yields, and NOT post-cost; (2) the sample is early/volatile crypto history (small caps, high returns) that may not generalize; (3) attention proxies are exactly the kind of signal that can look predictive in-sample and decay. Use as motivation to *test* momentum and (later) sentiment features through our robustness gate, not as a promise of returns.

### [13] Does the Stock Market Overreact?
- **Authors / Venue:** Werner F. M. De Bondt, Richard Thaler / Journal of Finance 40(3)
- **Year:** 1985
- **Source:** DOI:10.1111/j.1540-6261.1985.tb05004.x (primary paywalled; design/direction cross-read via StudyLib rendering + AlphaArchitect)
- **% read:** 40% (abstract + test design + qualitative result via secondary; primary paywalled and a clean source for the exact CAR magnitude was not retrieved, so no precise number asserted)
- **Summary:** The foundational **long-term reversal / overreaction** paper of behavioral finance. Rank US stocks by cumulative return over a prior 3–5 year *formation* period into "winner" and "loser" portfolios, then track the next 3–5 years. **Prior losers significantly outperform prior winners** over the subsequent 36 months (a large positive loser-minus-winner cumulative abnormal return), with an asymmetry that losers rebound more than winners give back. Attributed to investor **overreaction** to dramatic news (psychology), which subsequently corrects. Establishes the key term-structure of returns: **short/medium-horizon momentum (3–12m) coexists with long-horizon (3–5y) reversal** — the same horizons flagged in [1][2].
- **Relevance to our system:** Background/conceptual for a single coin. We can't form winner/loser portfolios, but the load-bearing lesson is the **horizon-dependence of the sign of autocorrelation**: trend over months, reversal over years. For our advisor this means a momentum signal's edge is *horizon-specific* and will invert if the lookback/holding window is mismatched to the regime — directly motivating that we (a) test multiple horizons and (b) never assume a window that trended will keep trending. It also conceptually pairs with mean-reversion strategies (the within-range cousin), and with [7]'s "combine opposites" theme. Not a deployable single-coin strategy.

### [14] A Century of Evidence on Trend-Following Investing
- **Authors / Venue:** Brian Hurst, Yao Hua Ooi, Lasse Heje Pedersen / Journal of Portfolio Management (AQR)
- **Year:** 2017
- **Source:** SSRN:2993026 ; PDF https://fairmodel.econ.yale.edu/ec439/hurst.pdf (binary)
- **% read:** 40% (abstract + construction + decade/crisis findings via SSRN abstract + WebSearch; primary PDFs binary, exact Sharpe not asserted from a clean source)
- **Summary:** Extends the time-series-momentum / trend-following (CTA) evidence back to **1880** (~135 years) across ~60 markets in equities, bonds, commodities, currencies. The strategy combines **1-, 3-, and 12-month** time-series momentum signals, **scaled to a constant target volatility** and equally risk-weighted across markets. Headline robustness claims: trend-following delivered **positive average returns in every decade since 1880**, with low correlation to traditional assets, and **performed well in 8 of the 10 largest crisis periods** (largest drawdowns of a 60/40 stock/bond portfolio) — i.e. it is a documented "crisis alpha"/long-volatility profile. Works across recessions/booms, war/peace, high/low rates and inflation. (Note: a separate strand of the literature argues trend returns have *weakened* in the low-vol post-2010s, and the paper's results are gross of the higher costs a retail trader pays.)
- **Relevance to our system:** Medium — the strongest *positive* evidence for trend-following as a robust style, and the clearest articulation of the recipe our advisor can test: **multi-horizon (1/3/12) trend signals + constant-vol scaling + diversification.** Two of those three transfer to one coin (multi-horizon blend, vol scaling); the third — diversification across 60 markets — is exactly what we *cannot* do and is plausibly responsible for much of the smooth, every-decade Sharpe. So the honest hypothesis: a single-coin, vol-scaled, multi-horizon trend rule captures the *signal* but loses the *diversification*, and may not beat buy-and-hold net of costs — precisely our thesis, and a clean bake-off experiment. The "crisis alpha" property is interesting for a long/flat advisor (trend goes flat/short into crashes), but on a single long-biased crypto position the value is muted.

### [15] Common Risk Factors in the Returns on Stocks and Bonds (Fama–French 3-factor)
- **Authors / Venue:** Eugene F. Fama, Kenneth R. French / Journal of Financial Economics 33(1)
- **Year:** 1993
- **Source:** DOI:10.1016/0304-405X(93)90023-5 ; model details via en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fama–French_three-factor_model
- **% read:** 40% (model + factor definitions + R² claim + caveats via Wikipedia; primary paywalled)
- **Summary:** The seminal multi-factor asset-pricing paper. Augments CAPM with two long-short factors: **SMB** (Small Minus Big — size) and **HML** (High Minus Low book-to-market — value), giving R = Rf + β(Rm−Rf) + s·SMB + h·HML + α. Empirically small-cap and high-book/market (value) stocks earn higher average returns than CAPM predicts, and the three-factor model **explains ~90%+ of diversified-portfolio return variance vs ~70% for CAPM**. Foundational to factor investing. Caveats acknowledged by later work: the size effect is weak/country-dependent and value has had long underperformance stretches.
- **Relevance to our system:** Background only. Factor models are *cross-sectional, multi-asset* pricing tools — a single-coin advisor has no cross-section to price, and crypto's "factors" [10][12] are an active, separate research area. The one durable methodological lesson: **a few systematic factors explain most return variation, and the rest is hard-to-capture idiosyncratic/alpha noise** — consistent with our thesis that most single-asset "edges" are noise. Also a reminder that even Nobel-grade factors (size, value) *decay and underperform for years*, reinforcing the robustness-gate mindset over chasing any one signal.

### [16] Statistical Arbitrage Pairs Trading Strategies: Review and Outlook
- **Authors / Venue:** Christopher Krauss / Journal of Economic Surveys 31(2)

- **% read:** 40% (full concept + the SUE-decile mechanism + the "anomalous anomaly" persistence + cost/decay caveats via the Caltech PDF extract + search summaries; exact return magnitude not cleanly recovered)
- **Summary:** **PEAD** (first seen in Ball–Brown 1968, named by Bernard–Thomas 1989) is the tendency for stock prices to **continue drifting in the direction of an earnings surprise for weeks/months** after the announcement: sort stocks by **standardized unexpected earnings (SUE)** into deciles, and the top-SUE decile keeps **outperforming** the bottom for ~60 trading days. Bernard–Thomas (1974–1986) found the top-minus-bottom SUE return spread was positive in **41 of 48 quarters.** The mechanism is **investor underreaction** — markets "fail to recognize fully the implications of current earnings for future earnings," so prices adjust gradually as the news diffuses. It is called the **"anomalous anomaly"** because it has **persisted for decades despite being maximally documented** — attributed to limits to arbitrage, transaction costs consuming the raw spread, and entrenched behavioral biases — though real-world profitability is materially reduced by costs and turnover, and the effect has weakened over time.
- **Relevance to our system:** **Background as a strategy (PEAD is an event-driven, fundamentals-based, cross-sectional equity anomaly — crypto has no quarterly earnings announcements or SUE, and we have no cross-section), but it earns a slot for two transferable lessons.** (1) PEAD is the **purest example of the underreaction-to-news mechanism** [13][49][61] that also underlies momentum — and the crypto analogue would be **drift after a discrete crypto "news/event"** (a major protocol upgrade, an exchange listing, a regulatory ruling, an earnings-like on-chain revenue print). Such event-drift is testable *only* with an exogenous event feed and is a prime **overfitting/multiple-testing trap** (few events, many ways to define the surprise [40][67]) — so it would need the same hard gate. (2) PEAD is the rare anomaly that **persisted despite publication** — the *exception* to the McLean–Pontiff decay [25] — precisely *because* costs/limits-to-arbitrage protect it; the lesson for us is the flip side: an edge survives only where it is **costly/hard to arbitrage**, which on a liquid coin at retail scale means *most* price-based edges have *no* such protection and decay [11][68]. Mandate: don't build event-drift without an event feed and a hard multiple-testing gate; use PEAD as the conceptual anchor for "underreaction to discrete news" if such a feed ever exists. Not single-coin-price-deployable.

### [81] Turn-of-the-Month Effect (Ariel 1987)
- **Authors / Venue:** Robert A. Ariel (1987, JFE) as the seminal source; refinements by Lakonishok–Smidt (1988), McConnell–Xu (2008)
- **Year:** 1987 (seminal); confirmations 1988–2008
- **Source:** Ariel DOI:10.1016/0304-405X(87)90066-3 ; McConnell-Xu open PDF https://www.chesler.us/resources/academia/turn_of_the_month_stock_returns.pdf ; https://quantpedia.com/strategies/turn-of-the-month-in-equity-indexes
- **% read:** 40% (full effect definition + the [-1:+8] window + the cash-flow mechanism + the recent-period confirmation via the Quantpedia/QuantSeeker/McConnell-Xu syntheses; primaries not all read as text)
- **Summary:** The **turn-of-the-month (TOM)** effect: equity returns are **abnormally concentrated around the month boundary.** Ariel (1987) showed nearly all of US equities' monthly excess return occurred in a **9-day window from the last trading day through the +8th** ([-1:+8]); Lakonishok–Smidt (1988) found a tighter [-1:+3] window captured **all** of the DJIA's positive return over 1897–1986; McConnell–Xu (2008) confirmed it **persisted over 1987–2005.** The leading mechanism is **institutional cash flows**: pension/retirement contributions and salaries are paid at month-end and deployed into equities early the next month, plus month-end fund rebalancing and window-dressing. Recent work links it to **infrequent rebalancing + risk deferral**, and finds it **larger after market turbulence and in recessions** (when investors hold more cash to deploy).
- **Relevance to our system:** **Background-leaning, and a sharp *negative* signpost for crypto — like the day-of-week effect [26][55], the TOM mechanism is equity-structural and largely absent in crypto.** The load-bearing point: TOM is driven by **monthly institutional cash-flow cycles** (payday/pension/salary deployment, month-end fund rebalancing, reporting-boundary window-dressing) — a **calendar structure that 24/7, retail-heavy, no-payroll-cycle crypto does not share** in the same way. So a naive "buy the turn of the month" overlay on a coin is a prime **data-mining/multiple-testing candidate** [42][32] with no mechanistic support, exactly the [55] lesson. Two nuances worth a hard-gated test only: (1) crypto *does* now have growing **institutional flows (ETFs [80], funds)** that could, eventually, import some month-end/rebalance seasonality — so the mechanism is not *impossible* post-institutionalization, but it is unestablished and recent; (2) the finding that TOM is **larger after turbulence / in recessions** ties to the risk-deferral idea. Mandate: **do not ship a turn-of-month overlay** without a mechanistic case and out-of-sample + multiple-testing survival; absent institutional-flow data it is almost certainly noise on crypto. Useful presenter contrast: "the turn-of-month rally is driven by payday and pension cash flows that 24/7 crypto doesn't have." Not single-coin-deployable.
