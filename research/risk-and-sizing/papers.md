### [1] Volatility Managed Portfolios
- **Authors / Venue:** Alan Moreira, Tyler Muir / The Journal of Finance 72(4), pp. 1611–1644 (NBER WP 22208)
- **Year:** 2017 (working paper 2015–2016)
- **Source:** SSRN:2659431; NBER w22208; PDF https://www.stern.nyu.edu/sites/default/files/assets/documents/Volatility%20Managed%20Portfolios.pdf
- **% read:** 55% (abstract + full intro + lit review + data-description section from PDF; results tables skimmed)
- **Summary:** The canonical pro-vol-timing paper. Construct "volatility managed" portfolios that scale each month's exposure by the inverse of last month's realized variance — f_t = (c/σ̂²_{t-1})·f, decreasing risk when variance is forecast high and vice versa. The motivating fact: variance is highly forecastable at horizons up to a year, but variance forecasts are only weakly related to future returns, so the conditional Sharpe E_t[R]/Var_t(R) rises when volatility falls. Applied to Mkt, Value, Momentum, Profitability (RMW), Investment (CMA), ROE, and the currency carry trade, the strategy earns large positive alphas and raises factor Sharpe ratios by ~50–100% of the original. They claim it survives transaction costs and works for most international indices, and that real-time parameter instability is negligible (contrast Goyal–Welch 2008 on return timing). Utility gains for a mean-variance investor are 50–90% of lifetime utility. A two-fund theorem holds: all investors hold a static mix of buy-and-hold + the vol-managed portfolio; short-horizon/mean-variance agents put ALL weight on the vol-managed fund.
- **Relevance to our system:** This is the intellectual basis of our vol-targeting overlay (scale exposure by 1/σ²). It is the "bull case" we must hold against the OOS critiques ([2] etc.). KEY for us: (a) even a crude "next month's vol = this month's realized vol" forecast is enough — matches our simple realized-vol overlay; (b) their headline gains are SPANNING-REGRESSION alphas, NOT a like-for-like return comparison, which is exactly the soft spot later papers attack; (c) the "all weight on the vol-managed fund" result is an in-sample, frictionless mean-variance claim. For our single-coin advisor we should treat Moreira–Muir as the hypothesis generator and the Cederburg-style OOS tests as the gate. Their own framing — vol is forecastable, returns are not — is the precise reason a vol overlay can cut drawdown/variance even if it does not robustly lift net Sharpe.

### [2] On the Performance of Volatility-Managed Portfolios
- **Authors / Venue:** Scott Cederburg, Michael S. O'Doherty, Feifei Wang, Xuemin (Sterling) Yan / Journal of Financial Economics 138(1), pp. 95–117
- **Year:** 2020
- **Source:** DOI 10.1016/j.jfineco.2020.04.015; PDF https://www.lehigh.edu/~xuy219/research/COWY.pdf
- **% read:** 70% (abstract + full intro + data/construction + direct-comparison section incl. Tables 1–2 from PDF; later robustness sections skimmed)
- **Summary:** The decisive out-of-sample rebuttal to Moreira–Muir. Tests 103 equity strategies (9 factors + 94 anomalies) and asks whether a REAL-TIME investor benefits from volatility management. Three findings. (1) Direct Sharpe comparisons of managed vs unmanaged: NO systematic advantage — the vol-managed version wins in only 53/103 cases, the original in 50/103, and only 8 differences are statistically significant (Table 2); the gains are concentrated in momentum-type strategies (MOM +8.23%/yr, ROE +2.86%, BAB +2.58% Sharpe-equivalent). For the market itself the Sharpe rises only 0.42→0.51 in-sample but the difference is borderline. (2) They REPRODUCE Moreira–Muir's positive spanning-regression alphas (77/103 positive, 23 significant), BUT show those alphas are NOT a real-time strategy: the regression f_{σ,t}=α+βf_t+ε requires combining the scaled and unscaled portfolio with weights that depend on IN-SAMPLE moments unknown until the end of the sample. A genuine real-time "combination" strategy (train on history, allocate OOS) UNDERPERFORMS simple investment in the original portfolio in 72/103 cases on certainty-equivalent return. Concrete: the OOS managed+unmanaged market combo earns Sharpe 0.42 vs 0.46 for just holding the unmanaged market. (3) The in-sample alpha does not translate OOS primarily because of STRUCTURAL INSTABILITY (parameter breaks) in the spanning regressions. Note the leverage problem: 99th-percentile required leverage exceeds 400% and reaches 864% for momentum.
- **Relevance to our system:** This is THE paper that justifies our app's skepticism and the OOS lesson baked into the brief. It cleanly separates the in-sample spanning-alpha (looks great) from the real-time deliverable (no robust Sharpe gain) — exactly the trap our FROZEN bootstrap gate + buy-and-hold benchmark are designed to catch. Direct lessons: (a) evaluate any vol overlay by DIRECT comparison vs the un-targeted baseline, never by an alpha/regression that secretly uses future info — this mirrors our mandatory baseline-equity-divergence e2e test and the buy-and-hold benchmark; (b) where vol management DID help it was on momentum/trend strategies, hinting our overlay is most likely to matter when stacked on a trend signal, not on buy-and-hold; (c) the 400–864% leverage requirement is a real implementability killer — for a long-only €200 advisor we cannot lever, so the up-scaling half of vol-targeting is mostly unavailable, leaving only the de-risking (drawdown-control) half; (d) "structural instability" is the crypto-relevant failure mode — regime breaks make the trained scaling stale.

### [3] Optimal Betting: Beyond the Long-Term Growth
- **Authors / Venue:** Levon Hakobyan, Sergey Lototsky / arXiv preprint (math.OC / q-fin.PM)
- **Year:** 2025
- **Source:** arXiv:2503.17927 (https://arxiv.org/abs/2503.17927)
- **% read:** 30% (abstract + intro framing via WebFetch; PDF math not fully parsed)
- **Summary:** Addresses the central practical weakness of the Kelly criterion: although the Kelly (log-optimal) portfolio maximizes long-term growth rate, it is an aggressive strategy whose wealth path has large fluctuations and deep interim drawdowns. The authors develop a unified risk-assessment framework based on the ASYMPTOTIC VARIANCE of the log-wealth process (in both discrete and continuous time), proposing two new variance-based risk measures that quantify growth-path volatility beyond the mean growth rate. These measures give a principled way to temper the Kelly fraction — i.e. motivate fractional Kelly — by trading a small reduction in expected growth for a substantial reduction in path variance.
- **Relevance to our system:** Reinforces the project-central "sizing is the lever" theme with the explicit caution that FULL Kelly is too aggressive to deploy: the growth-optimal bet has punishing variance/drawdown, so fractional Kelly (½ or less) is the deployable form. For our advisor this maps to: even if a strategy has positive edge, sizing at full-Kelly invites ruinous drawdowns; we should treat the fractional multiplier as a first-class risk knob and report path-variance, not just terminal growth. The asymptotic-variance lens is conceptually aligned with our bootstrap gate, which already looks at the dispersion of path outcomes, not just the point estimate.

### [4] Generalized Framework for Applying the Kelly Criterion to Stock Markets
- **Authors / Venue:** Tim Byrnes, Tristan Barnett / arXiv preprint (q-fin.PM)
- **Year:** 2018
- **Source:** arXiv:1806.05293 (https://arxiv.org/abs/1806.05293)
- **% read:** 65% (abstract + intro + full single-stock and multi-stock derivations from PDF; correlated-stock section + examples skimmed)
- **Summary:** Provides a clean, computable recipe for Kelly fractions when returns are continuous (stocks) rather than binary bets. Given an arbitrary probability distribution for next-period prices, the optimal Kelly fraction depends only on the first and second moments: for a single asset, f = (⟨x⟩/x₀ − 1)/(1 + ⟨x²⟩/x₀² − 2⟨x⟩/x₀). For geometric Brownian motion this reduces, for small μ,σ, to the standard f ≈ μ/σ² (growth over variance); the exact log-normal form f = (e^μ−1)/(1+e^{2μ+σ²}−2e^μ) gives systematically SMALLER, more conservative fractions than μ/σ² as volatility rises (Fig. 1). For a portfolio of L assets the Kelly fractions solve a linear system f = M⁻¹b involving only the mean vector and covariance matrix; positively correlated assets reduce each fraction, negatively correlated assets increase them (diversification). Fractions can exceed 1 (leverage) for high growth.
- **Relevance to our system:** Gives the exact sizing formula our single-coin advisor would use if it sized by Kelly: f ≈ μ/σ² with μ,σ estimated from the (coin,window). Three takeaways. (1) The σ² in the denominator means Kelly sizing IS a form of volatility targeting — high vol → smaller position — so our vol-targeting overlay is a crude Kelly. (2) The exact (non-Gaussian) formula is MORE conservative than μ/σ² precisely when vol is high and tails fat, which is the crypto regime — so naive μ/σ² over-bets on crypto. (3) The whole edifice rests on knowing μ (expected return), which is exactly the quantity that is nearly unforecastable for a single coin (cf. our validated "no robust edge"); since Kelly is extremely sensitive to the μ estimate, garbage-in μ → over-leveraged garbage-out. This is the bridge to the estimation-risk papers and a strong argument for fractional Kelly + hard caps in any sizing we ship.

### [5] Tackling Estimation Risk in Kelly Investing Using Options
- **Authors / Venue:** Fabrizio Lillo, Piero Mazzarisi, Ioanna-Yvonni Tsaknaki / arXiv preprint (q-fin)
- **Year:** 2025
- **Source:** arXiv:2508.18868 (https://arxiv.org/abs/2508.18868)
- **% read:** 25% (abstract + framing via WebFetch)
- **Summary:** Tackles the best-known practical flaw of Kelly investing: the optimal-fraction condition is highly sensitive to accurate estimates of probabilities and payoffs, so estimation error produces greatly suboptimal (often over-levered) portfolios. The authors show, in a simplified binomial model, that adding European OPTIONS to the asset menu lets one construct growth-optimal portfolios that are ROBUST to estimation errors — the convex option payoff caps downside and reduces the penalty from mis-estimating the return distribution.
- **Relevance to our system:** Background-to-adjacent. The headline lesson — Kelly's estimation fragility — directly reinforces why our advisor should NOT size at full Kelly from a noisy single-coin μ̂. The specific fix (options to hedge estimation risk) is mostly out of scope for a spot-only €200 paper advisor (no options book), but the conceptual point — a convex, capped payoff is more forgiving of a bad return estimate — is a relevance note for any future tail-hedge or stop-loss overlay: bounded-downside structures buy robustness against the very forecast errors our thesis says are unavoidable.

### [6] Good and Bad Properties of the Kelly Criterion
- **Authors / Venue:** Leonard C. MacLean, Edward O. Thorp, William T. Ziemba / in "The Kelly Capital Growth Investment Criterion" (World Scientific, 2011); working paper
- **Year:** 2010
- **Source:** PDF https://www.stat.berkeley.edu/~aldous/157/Papers/Good_Bad_Kelly.pdf; book SSRN:1797366
- **% read:** 80% (full 4-page summary read: abstract, both figures, all enumerated good/bad properties, fractional-Kelly formula)
- **Summary:** The authoritative plain-language summary by the field's three leading authors. GOOD: maximizing E[log wealth] period-by-period maximizes the asymptotic exponential growth rate and (asymptotically) the Kelly bettor's wealth exceeds that of any "essentially different" strategy with probability →1, minimizing expected time to reach a wealth goal. BAD: because log utility has near-zero Arrow-Pratt risk aversion (R_A = 1/w), suggested bets can be huge and the strategy is very risky short-term — a long run of bad outcomes can still wipe out most capital over any finite horizon. Two crucial quantitative facts. (1) Sizing inputs are wildly asymmetric in importance: errors in the MEAN are ~20× as costly as errors in covariances and ~10× as costly as errors in variances (Chopra–Ziemba 1993; Table 1, ratios 20:10:2 in cash-equivalent loss), and the lower the risk aversion the worse mean-errors bite. (2) It NEVER pays to bet more than full Kelly — overbetting raises risk AND lowers growth; betting exactly 2× Kelly drives the long-run growth rate down to the risk-free rate (continuous approx). Remedy = FRACTIONAL Kelly (blend Kelly with cash): for log-normal returns, betting fraction α of Kelly is exactly optimal for the negative-power utility δw^δ with α = 1/(1−δ), so half-Kelly ↔ δ=−1, quarter-Kelly ↔ δ=−3. They estimate Buffett behaved like a full-Kelly bettor (no borrowing), Keynes like an 80% Kelly bettor.
- **Relevance to our system:** Extremely actionable and almost a design spec for our sizing knob. The 20:10:2 result is the single most important sizing lesson for us: our advisor's μ̂ for a single coin is the LEAST reliable input yet the MOST consequential for any growth-optimal sizing — so a Kelly-style sizer is most exposed exactly where we are weakest, strongly arguing for heavy fractional shrinkage (½ or ¼ Kelly) and/or refusing μ-dependent sizing in favor of vol-only (variance/covariance) sizing where estimation error is far cheaper. "Never bet more than Kelly; 2× Kelly → risk-free growth" is a hard upper guardrail for any leverage/sizing feature. The fractional-Kelly↔negative-power-utility map gives us a principled dial: pick δ for the operator's risk tolerance rather than an ad-hoc multiplier. Confirms the project thesis from the sizing side: growth-optimal sizing is real but its fragility (mean-estimation + finite-horizon ruin) is why naive deployment loses.

### [7] Equally-Weighted Risk Contribution Portfolios: An Empirical Study Using Expected Shortfall
- **Authors / Venue:** Elisabetta Cagna, Giulio Casuccio / CeRP Working Paper 142/14
- **Year:** 2014
- **Source:** PDF https://fileserver.carloalberto.org/cerp/WP_142.pdf
- **% read:** 55% (abstract + intro + lit review + ERC/ES methodology incl. risk-contribution formulas from PDF; empirical tables skimmed)
- **Summary:** Extends the Maillard–Roncalli–Teiletche (2009) Equal-Risk-Contribution (ERC / "risk parity") portfolio from a standard-deviation risk measure to EXPECTED SHORTFALL (ES, a coherent tail-risk measure), with risk contributions estimated via a filtered BOOTSTRAP to reduce historical-sample estimation error and capture non-Gaussian tails. ERC chooses weights so each asset's risk contribution RC_i = w_i·∂ρ/∂w_i is equal across assets; this requires the risk measure ρ be linear-homogeneous in weights (both σ and ES qualify) so total risk decomposes additively. Restates the standard result that the ERC portfolio sits between minimum-variance and equal-weight, and that min-variance has higher Sharpe but higher short-run drawdowns. Empirically (12 instruments, 1999–2011, with transaction costs) they find ERC portfolios are SUPERIOR — better Sharpe ratio and better asymmetric (tail) performance metrics than equal-weight.
- **Relevance to our system:** Mostly background for a SINGLE-coin advisor (risk parity is inherently a multi-asset diversification tool — with one risky asset there is nothing to equalize risk across). Two transferable ideas. (1) The risk-CONTRIBUTION decomposition mindset and the use of EXPECTED SHORTFALL (coherent, tail-aware) over plain standard deviation is the right way to think about crypto's fat-tailed risk — if we ever report risk for our pick, ES/CVaR beats σ. (2) Their use of a FILTERED BOOTSTRAP to de-noise risk estimates and handle non-Gaussian returns directly parallels our 1000-path moving-block bootstrap gate — independent endorsement that resampling is the right tool for honest risk numbers on heavy-tailed data. Note for later: a "coin vs cash" two-state ERC (equalize risk contribution between the coin position and the cash buffer) IS a degenerate way to think about position sizing — worth a mention but not core.

### [8] When Do Stop-Loss Rules Stop Losses?
- **Authors / Venue:** Kathryn M. Kaminski, Andrew W. Lo / Journal of Financial Markets 18, pp. 234–254 (EFA 2007 Ljubljana)
- **Year:** 2014 (working paper 2007)
- **Source:** SSRN:968338 (https://papers.ssrn.com/sol3/papers.cfm?abstract_id=968338) — SSRN returned 403; DOI 10.1016/j.finmar.2013.07.001
- **% read:** 25% (abstract + result via multiple consistent secondary sources [search snippets, the Zambelli paper that cites it]; SSRN/ScienceDirect full text blocked)
- **Summary:** The canonical theoretical treatment of stop-loss efficacy. Builds a framework for how a simple "exit after cumulative losses exceed a threshold, sit in the safe asset, re-enter later" stop-loss rule changes a strategy's expected return and volatility. KEY RESULT: under the Random Walk Hypothesis (IID returns) a stop-loss rule ALWAYS REDUCES expected return — you systematically sell low and miss the rebound, paying a "stopping premium" for nothing. Stop-losses only ADD value ("positive stopping premium") when returns have MOMENTUM / positive serial correlation (losses tend to be followed by more losses) or regime structure, so exiting avoids a continuing downtrend. Empirically, applied to a switch between US equities and long-term bonds (monthly 1950–2004), a stop-loss improved returns by ~50–100 bps and reduced volatility, consistent with momentum/flight-to-safety dynamics.
- **Relevance to our system:** Directly load-bearing for our "no robust edge" thesis and for any stop-loss feature. The clean theorem — stop-losses are value-DESTROYING under a random walk and only help under momentum — means that for a buy-and-hold benchmark on a near-random-walk coin, adding a stop-loss should be EXPECTED to lower return (sell-low / miss-rebound), exactly matching our finding that active overlays rarely beat holding. A stop-loss is therefore a DRAWDOWN/volatility-reduction tool with a return COST, not a free lunch — and only earns its keep if the coin/window actually trends. This argues we should (a) only consider stop-losses stacked on a trend signal, not on buy-and-hold; (b) measure the stopping premium (return change) AND the drawdown reduction separately and let the operator choose; (c) be skeptical of any backtest where a stop-loss "improves" returns — check whether it is just fitting realized momentum.

### [9] Determining Optimal Stop-Loss Thresholds via Bayesian Analysis of Drawdown Distributions
- **Authors / Venue:** Antoine E. Zambelli / arXiv preprint (q-fin.RM)
- **Year:** 2016
- **Source:** arXiv:1609.00869 (https://arxiv.org/abs/1609.00869)
- **% read:** 90% (full 6-page paper + both data tables read via PDF)
- **Summary:** Proposes a systematic, data-driven way to SET the stop-loss threshold (rather than picking 3%/5% arbitrarily). Method: run a signal-only SMA strategy (long when price > 20-period SMA), record the maximum drawdown of every round-trip trade, bin the drawdowns, and tag each as ending in a "win" or "loss". From the conditional drawdown histograms estimate E[return | drawdown ≤ threshold] and choose the threshold T = argmax of that expected return — i.e. the trailing-stop level that historically best separated rebounders from continued losers. Two variants: a "T method" using only realized trades and an "R method" using a denser rolling-window of synthetic trades. HONEST RESULTS: tested on 114 liquid US ETFs/stocks (hourly, 2014–2016), the optimized stop beat the no-stop signal-only baseline in only ~51.75% (T) to 57.02% (R) of names, with average gains +6.37% / +7.16% but average LOSSES −6.94% / −8.20% in the cases where it hurt — an overall barely-positive-to-negative expected change. Explicitly cites Kaminski–Lo that stops are not always beneficial.
- **Relevance to our system:** A concrete, portable recipe AND a sobering result. The method (fit the drawdown distribution, pick the stop that maximizes conditional expected return) is exactly the kind of principled, in-app-computable rule we could add — and crucially it's evaluated by DIRECT comparison vs the no-stop baseline, the same discipline as our gate. But the result is the real lesson: even a carefully optimized, trailing stop helps in barely over half of assets and its winning and losing cases roughly cancel — strong corroboration that stop-losses are not a robust edge. The author's own caveats (data sparsity from infrequent trades → spurious bins; "going too far back makes the data stale" = regime drift) mirror our overfitting and structural-instability concerns. Bottom line for us: if we ship a stop, derive its level from the drawdown distribution rather than a magic number, but expect near-zero net benefit and surface drawdown reduction as the real (only) payoff.

### [10] Optimal Investment Strategies for Controlling Drawdowns
- **Authors / Venue:** Sanford J. Grossman, Zhongquan Zhou / Mathematical Finance 3(3), pp. 241–276
- **Year:** 1993
- **Source:** DOI 10.1111/j.1467-9965.1993.tb00044.x (https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/abs/10.1111/j.1467-9965.1993.tb00044.x)
- **% read:** 25% (abstract + result via multiple consistent secondary sources; paywalled full text not retrieved)
- **Summary:** The foundational continuous-time paper on drawdown-constrained investing. An investor wants wealth W_t to never fall below a fixed fraction α of its running maximum M_t (the high-water mark): constraint W_t ≥ α·M_t, with α∈(0,1) the maximum acceptable drawdown. Grossman–Zhou solve, in a lognormal (Black–Scholes) market with one risky and one risk-free asset, for the policy maximizing long-run growth of expected (CRRA) utility subject to this constraint. KEY RESULT: the optimal risky allocation is PROPORTIONAL TO THE "CUSHION"/SURPLUS (W_t − α·M_t) — invest aggressively when far above the floor, de-risk toward zero risky exposure as wealth approaches the drawdown floor. This is the theoretical parent of CPPI with a stochastic (high-water-mark) floor, and the strategy that minimizes probability of drawdown coincides with the one that minimizes probability of ruin. (Later work — Cvitanić–Karatzas 1995; Klass–Nowicki 2005 — extends it and shows the GZ rule is not always optimal in discrete time.)
- **Relevance to our system:** This is the principled blueprint for a drawdown-control overlay in our advisor: scale the coin position by the distance between current equity and a high-water-mark floor (e.g. "never lose more than 20% of peak paper-equity"). For a long-only €200 account that CAN go to cash, the GZ rule is directly implementable as a sell-down-as-you-approach-the-floor schedule (the down-side is available even though leverage is not). Crucially it gives the operator a HARD, interpretable risk promise ("max drawdown ≈ α") which is far more useful to a retail user than a Sharpe number. Caveat: GZ assumes lognormal/continuous prices — crypto's jumps create GAP RISK (see CPPI papers) so the realized floor can be breached; and it is a pure risk-control device, expected to cost return (you de-risk into recoveries), consistent with our thesis. Worth bake-off testing as a drawdown tool, not a return enhancer.

### [11] Value-at-Risk-Based Portfolio Insurance: Performance Evaluation and Benchmarking Against CPPI in a Markov-Modulated Regime-Switching Market
- **Authors / Venue:** Peyman Alipour, Ali Foroush Bastani / arXiv preprint (q-fin)
- **Year:** 2023
- **Source:** arXiv:2305.12539 (https://arxiv.org/abs/2305.12539)
- **% read:** 25% (abstract + findings via WebFetch)
- **Summary:** Compares two portfolio-insurance (capital-protection) strategies in a regime-switching market where volatility and expected returns jump between hidden Markov states: (1) VaR-Based Portfolio Insurance (VBPI), which sets risky exposure so a Value-at-Risk floor is respected, vs (2) CPPI (Constant Proportion Portfolio Insurance), the standard benchmark that holds risky-asset exposure = multiplier × cushion above a floor. Evaluated with Sharpe, Omega, and Kappa ratios across regimes, they find CPPI has the better risk-return tradeoff in most scenarios and more stable terminal returns.
- **Relevance to our system:** Background-to-adjacent. Portfolio insurance (CPPI/VBPI) is the institutional cousin of the GZ drawdown rule [10] and of our "go to cash to protect capital" instinct, and this paper's regime-switching setting is exactly the crypto-relevant context. Two takeaways: (1) CPPI's cushion-multiplier rule is simple and robust enough to beat a fancier VaR-based scheme even under regime switching — favoring SIMPLE drawdown rules over estimation-heavy ones (consistent with [2][6]); (2) it implicitly flags GAP RISK as the key failure mode — between rebalances a jump larger than cushion/multiplier breaches the floor, which for a jumpy crypto coin is a live risk any capital-protection overlay we build must account for (discrete rebalancing on a gapping asset cannot truly guarantee the floor).

### [12] Multi-Period Portfolio Selection with Drawdown Control
- **Authors / Venue:** Peter Nystrup, Stephen Boyd, Erik Lindström, Henrik Madsen / Annals of Operations Research 282, pp. 245–271
- **Year:** 2019 (online 2018)
- **Source:** DOI 10.1007/s10479-018-2947-3; author copy PDF (Stanford/Boyd) https://web.stanford.edu/~boyd/papers/pdf/multiperiod_portfolio_drawdown.pdf
- **% read:** 55% (abstract + intro + full MPC formulation + risk/drawdown-control sections incl. the γ_t rule from PDF; empirical results section skimmed)
- **Summary:** Implements drawdown control inside a multi-period (model-predictive-control, MPC) portfolio optimizer. Each period it forecasts mean and covariance from a hidden Markov model (regime-switching), then solves a convex problem maximizing risk-adjusted return net of transaction/holding costs over a short planning horizon H, executes the first trade, and rolls forward. Drawdown control is implemented elegantly: instead of a hard, non-convex max-drawdown constraint, they RAMP UP RISK AVERSION as drawdown deepens — γ_t = γ_0 · D^max/(D^max − D_t), where D_t = 1 − V_t/M_t is the drawdown from the high-water mark M_t and D^max is the limit; as D_t → D^max risk aversion → ∞ so risky exposure → 0. This is the convex, "soft" cousin of the Grossman–Zhou cushion rule [10] and of CPPI. HEADLINE RESULT: tested out-of-sample over two decades on indices mimicking major liquid asset classes, the approach "successfully controls drawdowns with little or no sacrifice of mean–variance efficiency," and with leverage one can raise return without raising max drawdown. They note transaction/holding costs double as estimation-error regularizers, and explicitly warn that getting "trapped" near the drawdown limit can be inescapable if the limit is too tight.
- **Relevance to our system:** The most directly implementable drawdown-control recipe found so far, and the most encouraging result for an overlay. The γ_t = γ_0·D^max/(D^max − D_t) rule is a clean, single-line way to add an operator-set max-drawdown promise to our advisor: convert "I'll tolerate at most a 25% drawdown of my €200" into a position-shrinking schedule that de-risks as paper-equity falls toward the floor — no leverage required (we only need the de-risking direction). The "little or no sacrifice of mean-variance efficiency" finding is the one genuine ray of hope that a risk overlay need not always cost return, but note it's IN THIS multi-asset, regime-forecasting setting; for a single coin with no forecastable μ the result may not carry, so we must verify by DIRECT comparison vs buy-and-hold (our gate). Also strongly endorses: (a) transaction costs as a regularizer (we already charge costs), (b) regime-switching/HMM as the right model for the structural instability that broke vol-timing [2]. The "trapped at the limit" warning is a concrete UX caveat: a too-tight floor can lock the user out of recovery.

### [13] On Drawdown-Modulated Feedback Control in Stock Trading
- **Authors / Venue:** Chung-Han Hsieh, B. Ross Barmish / arXiv preprint (math.OC); IFAC context
- **Year:** 2017
- **Source:** arXiv:1710.01503 (https://arxiv.org/abs/1710.01503)
- **% read:** 80% (full 6-page paper incl. the Drawdown Modulation Lemma, the Tesla numerical example, and both in/out-of-sample figures from PDF)
- **Summary:** Casts drawdown control as a feedback-control problem: investment I(k) = γ·M(k)·V(k), a time-varying-gain controller on account value V(k). Their DRAWDOWN MODULATION LEMMA gives a necessary-and-sufficient bound on I(k) so that percentage drawdown d(k) = 1 − V(k)/V_max(k) stays ≤ a prescribed d_max with probability one, via the modulator M(k) = (d_max − d(k))/(1 − d(k)) — as current drawdown approaches the limit, M(k) → 0 and the position is forced toward zero. They then solve a DRAWDOWN-CONSTRAINED KELLY problem (maximize expected log-growth subject to the modulation), recovering classical Kelly as the special case d_max → 1. NUMERICAL EXAMPLE on Tesla (2013–2014): the drawdown-modulated Kelly with d_max = 5% held realized out-of-sample drawdown to ~5% (vs the constraint) and terminal value 1.005×, whereas UNCONSTRAINED classical Kelly grew to 1.136× but suffered a 22.5% drawdown — a textbook illustration of the return-for-drawdown trade. Notably, when the drawdown limit is hit the position goes to zero, so the controller automatically behaves like a STOP-LOSS. Generalizes to n-asset portfolios.

- **Summary:** The rigorous mathematical FOUNDATION of the Kelly criterion — the theorem that underwrites every growth-optimal / log-optimal sizing entry in this ledger ([3][4][6][20][42][80]). Kelly (1956) proposed maximizing E[log wealth]; Breiman (1961) PROVED its optimality with two landmark asymptotic results for repeated favorable bets. (1) MAGNITUDE / DOMINANCE: the log-optimal (Kelly) strategy maximizes the asymptotic exponential rate of capital growth, and asymptotically the Kelly bettor's wealth EXCEEDS that of any "essentially different" strategy with probability → 1 (the Kelly portfolio almost surely outgrows any other in the long run). (2) TIME: the Kelly strategy asymptotically MINIMIZES the expected time to reach a fixed large wealth goal. Together these establish log-utility maximization as the uniquely growth-optimal long-run criterion (later extended by Thorp 1969 and the distributionally-robust version [42]).
- **Relevance to our system:** The capstone theory anchor for the whole "sizing is the lever" topic — and, read honestly, a precise statement of WHY Kelly's guarantee does not rescue a single-coin advisor. Breiman's optimality is ASYMPTOTIC ("with probability → 1," "in the long run") and PRESUMES a genuinely FAVORABLE game with a KNOWN edge — both assumptions fail for us: (1) our holding window has far too few "bets" to reach the asymptotic regime ([55] quantified this: Kelly needs ~10k–40k trades to reliably dominate; a few-year single-coin window has orders of magnitude fewer), so the dominance theorem is unavailable and finite-horizon variance/ruin dominates; (2) our validated "no robust single-coin edge" thesis means the game is NOT reliably favorable and the edge is NOT known — and Breiman's proof says nothing about an over-bet on a mis-estimated or zero edge except (via the same log-utility math) that it destroys growth ([6][20]). So the deepest theoretical justification for growth-optimal sizing simultaneously delineates its inapplicability to our setting, which is exactly why our app defaults to vol-based risk-shaping (μ̂≈0 ⇒ Kelly→0 ⇒ "don't actively over-bet; hold") rather than μ-driven Kelly sizing. A foundational citation anchor: it is the bedrock the entire ledger rests on, and its asymptotic, known-edge premises are precisely what a single-coin retail advisor lacks. Pure background, but the right note to close the topic on — growth-optimal sizing is real and proven, and proven to be the wrong tool for our finite-horizon, no-edge, no-leverage problem.
